Tolerance Stack-Up: When RSS Fails and Monte Carlo Saves the Design

Jul 24, 2026 8 min read By SectorCalc Engineering Team Reviewed by Prof. Dr. Neela Nataraj, IIT Bombay

Every mechanical engineer learns root-sum-square in school. Square the tolerances, add them, take the square root. Elegant, fast, and — in many cases — dangerously wrong.

RSS assumes every contributor is independent, centered, and normally distributed with the stated tolerance representing ±3σ. That assumption collapses the moment a single component deviates from normality. And in real manufacturing, they almost always do.

Why RSS Works (When It Works)

RSS is derived from the central limit theorem. When you sum many independent random variables, the distribution of the sum approaches normal — regardless of the individual distributions. The variance of the sum equals the sum of the variances:

σ_stack² = σ₁² + σ₂² + ... + σₙ²

If every tolerance ti represents ±3σi, then the RSS spread is:

T_RSS = √(t₁² + t₂² + ... + tₙ²)

This works beautifully when:

  • All processes are capable (Cpk ≥ 1.33) and centered
  • Variations are truly independent
  • Sample sizes are large enough for the CLT to dominate
  • No contributor has hard physical limits (truncation)

Where RSS Breaks Down

The problems start when any contributor violates the normality assumption. Here are the four most common failure modes:

1. Uniform Distribution — No Central Tendency

A rough casting wall thickness, an uncontrolled shim pack, or a legacy ± tolerance block without capability data often behaves uniformly. Every value between nominal − t and nominal + t is equally likely. RSS treats this as ±3σ normal, but uniform has heavier tails than normal at the same total spread. The result: RSS underestimates the true 99.7% envelope.

2. Truncated Normal — 100% Screening

When inspection physically rejects out-of-limit parts, the distribution is not normal — it's truncated. The tails are cut off. RSS cannot model this because it assumes the full ±3σ spread is always present. Truncated normal reduces effective variance, but RSS doesn't know that.

3. Triangular — Expert Elicitation

When you know the minimum, maximum, and most likely value but lack sample data, triangular is the pragmatic choice. More conservative than normal at the tails, less conservative than uniform. RSS has no concept of triangular shape.

4. Mixed Distributions — The Real World

Most stacks contain a mix: a turned shaft (normal), a cast housing (uniform), a shim pack (triangular), and a screened bearing (truncated normal). RSS forces everything into a single normal model, losing the shape information that drives tail risk.

Monte Carlo: The Distribution-Agnostic Solution

Monte Carlo simulation doesn't assume a closed-form solution. It draws random samples from each contributor's assigned distribution, sums them, and builds an empirical histogram. With 10,000 iterations, you get:

  • The full empirical distribution of the stack
  • Exact P0.13 and P99.87 percentiles (the 99.7% envelope)
  • Predicted PPM outside specification limits
  • 95% confidence interval on the defect rate
  • Contributor Pareto — which part drives the most variation

Crucially, Monte Carlo lets you assign the correct distribution per contributor. Normal for the lathe, uniform for the casting, triangular for the shim, truncated-normal for the screened bearing. The simulation respects each shape.

Determinism: The Engineering Requirement

Monte Carlo has a reputation for being "random" and therefore non-reproducible. That reputation is earned by tools that don't fix the seed. Practical Decision Framework

Scenario Method Rationale
All normal, capable, independent RSS Fast, conservative enough
Any non-normal contributor Monte Carlo Shape matters for tail risk
Safety-critical, single failure path Worst-case Guaranteed closure at extremes
Mixed distributions, cost optimization Monte Carlo Quantify tolerance relief potential

Conclusion

RSS is not wrong — it's just incomplete. It answers a specific question under specific assumptions. When those assumptions break, Monte Carlo is the only method that preserves the engineering truth of your stack. The cost is computation time (milliseconds for 10,000 iterations), but the benefit is a defensible, reproducible prediction that survives audit.

"The best stack-up analyst runs all three methods. If WC fails, the design is not robust. If RSS and Monte Carlo disagree, your distribution assumptions need work. If all three agree, you have a story worth telling the customer."
